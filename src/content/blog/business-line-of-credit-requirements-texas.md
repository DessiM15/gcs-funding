Most business owners find out what they needed for a line of credit **after** being declined. That is a bad way to learn it, because a decline sits on the file and the next lender sees it.

Here are the actual thresholds, what each one is really measuring, and what happens when you miss one.

## The baseline requirements

For the revolving lines we place, the qualifying floor is:

- **600+ FICO score**
- **6+ months in business**
- **$100,000 in annual revenue**

To apply, you need basic details about you and the business, plus either a bank connection or your three most recent bank statements. Approvals come back in as fast as 20 minutes, and funds from a draw reach your account within hours.

That is genuinely the whole list. What is worth understanding is why each item is on it.

## What each requirement is actually measuring

### Credit score: your track record, not your character

A 600 floor is lower than most banks and higher than nothing. Lenders use it as a fast proxy for repayment history. It is also the requirement with the most flexibility built around it, because a strong revenue picture can offset a mediocre score in ways a weak revenue picture cannot offset a strong one.

### Time in business: survival odds

Six months is a screening line, not a judgment. Business failure rates are heavily front-loaded, so a lender pricing a revolving facility wants to see you past the steepest part of that curve.

### Revenue: the actual repayment source

This is the one that matters most, and it is the one owners underestimate. $100,000 in annual revenue is the threshold, but **consistency** matters as much as the total. Three months of bank statements showing steady deposits will do more for a marginal file than a single strong quarter.

## What a line of credit costs when you are not using it

Nothing, and this is the part worth internalising.

There are no fees to open the line, no monthly maintenance fees, no prepayment penalties, and no account closure fees. You pay for what you draw, and the available credit replenishes as you repay.

Which leads to the single most useful piece of advice in this article:

> Open the line when you do not need it. The month you need capital is the month your numbers look worst, and that is the month underwriting is hardest.

An unused line costs you nothing to carry. An unavailable line costs you the opportunity.

## If you fall short on one requirement

Missing one threshold is not the end of the conversation, it just changes which lender the file goes to. This is the practical difference between working with a single lender and working with a broker holding more than 20 lender relationships across every credit tier.

Common paths when the line of credit box does not fit:

- **Under 6 months in business.** Equipment and commercial truck financing has second-chance programs specifically for startups, usually with a larger down payment.
- **Credit below 600.** Our lender pool reaches well below that for asset-backed and consumer products, including files with bankruptcies, tax liens, judgments, and repossessions.
- **Revenue under $100,000.** If you sell to consumers, a [customer financing program](/partners) often solves the underlying problem better than borrowing does, because it grows revenue instead of adding debt.

That last point deserves emphasis. Plenty of businesses looking for working capital are really trying to solve a sales problem. If your customers cannot pay in full today, financing them costs you nothing to set up and pays you within 24 hours, while a loan puts the obligation on your balance sheet instead.
