Ask a contractor why they would offer customer financing and almost all of them give the same answer: to close more jobs. That is true, and it is the smaller half of the benefit.

The larger effect is on the jobs you were going to close anyway.

## The mechanism: buyers evaluate payments, not totals

When a homeowner is handed a $38,000 estimate, they evaluate a $38,000 decision. Their brain compares it against savings, other planned spending, and a general sense of what is reasonable to spend on one thing. Almost everything optional in that estimate is at risk.

When the same homeowner is handed a monthly payment, they evaluate a monthly decision. And this is the important part: **the upgrades are cheap in monthly terms.**

The difference between the base system and the better one might be $6,000 on the total. Spread across a term, it is a small number per month, against a benefit they actually want. The upgrade survives. On the lump-sum estimate, it is the first thing cut.

Multiply that across every proposal and the average ticket moves.

## What that looks like in real numbers

Take a remodeler running 30 estimates a month at a $12,000 average ticket and a 30% close rate.

**Today:** 30 × 30% × $12,000 = **$108,000 a month**

Now assume two conservative changes. Financing recovers deals lost purely on price, lifting close rate by 8 points to 38%. And keeping upgrades in scope lifts average ticket by 15%, to $13,800.

**With financing:** 30 × 38% × $13,800 = **$157,320 a month**

That is roughly **$590,000 a year** in additional revenue, from the same lead flow and the same crews. You can run your own figures in the [financing impact calculator](/resources/financing-impact-calculator).

Note which lever did more work. The close rate lift added about $28,000 a month. The ticket lift added about $21,000. Neither dominates, and most contractors only ever think about the first one.

## Why the objection is usually price, even when it does not sound like it

"Let me talk to my spouse." "We're getting a couple more quotes." "Send it over and I'll look at it."

Some of those are genuine. Many are a polite version of *that is more than we can spend right now*. Customers rarely say the real thing, because saying it out loud is uncomfortable.

The tell is what happens next: they go quiet, or they come back asking what can be removed. Both are price, arriving in disguise.

A monthly payment option, presented before the objection rather than after it, changes the conversation from *can we afford this* to *which payment works*. That is a much easier question for a customer to say yes to.

## Presenting it so it actually works

Three things separate programs that move the number from programs that sit unused:

**Present it with the estimate, not after the objection.** Once someone has said no, offering financing reads as a rescue attempt. Presented alongside the total from the start, it reads as a normal way to buy.

**Show the payment difference on upgrades specifically.** Not just the payment on the total. The upgrade conversation is where the ticket lift lives.

**Let them apply on their own phone.** Applications are 100% paperless and work on any device. Privacy matters more than people admit, particularly for customers who are not certain they will be approved.
